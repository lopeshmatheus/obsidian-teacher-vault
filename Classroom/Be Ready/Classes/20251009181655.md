### Present perfect vs past simple tenses

Extensive discussion on when to use "have/has + past participle" versus simple past tense.

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that present perfect is used when the time period hasn't finished or when timing is unspecified; past simple is used with specific time markers like "yesterday" or "last year"; emphasized learning through pattern recognition rather than complex rules; provided multiple examples including "I can't find my keys, I think I have lost them" vs "I lost my keys yesterday"
    - leticia.zach: Initially confused about when to use each tense; struggled with "I went to Korea last year" vs "I have been to Korea"; gradually understood through repeated examples; recognized that "lately" and "recently" trigger present perfect usage
- Conclusion
    
    - Use present perfect when the action's timing is unspecified or the time period is ongoing
    - Use past simple with specific time markers (yesterday, last year, last week)
    - Certain words like "lately," "recently," and "this week" typically require present perfect
    - Pattern recognition and exposure are more effective than memorizing grammatical rules

### "Have been" vs "had been" usage

Discussion on the distinction between present perfect and past perfect forms of "to be."

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that "have been to" indicates someone went somewhere and returned; "have gone to" means they are still there; "had been" is used when comparing two past events; provided examples: "My mother has been to Korea recently" (she's back) vs "My mother has gone to Korea recently" (she's still there)
    - leticia.zach: Successfully understood and applied the distinction after examples; correctly used "had been working for 5 years before he decided to leave"
- Conclusion
    
    - "Have/has been to" indicates completed travel with return
    - "Have/has gone to" indicates the person is still at the destination
    - "Had been" is used when one past action occurred before another past action

### Past perfect tense usage

Detailed explanation of when and how to use past perfect tense.

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that past perfect is used when comparing two past events, with the earlier event using "had + past participle"; emphasized that "by the time" followed by past tense signals past perfect in the next clause; clarified that specific time expressions like "before the end of the month" don't require past perfect unless comparing with another past action; noted that "went" is never correct - it's either "gone" or "been"
    - leticia.zach: Completed quiz questions correctly identifying: "By the time I arrived at the party, my friends had already left"; "Before I traveled, I had saved money for 3 months"; "He had been working for that company for 5 years before he decided to leave"
- Conclusion
    
    - Past perfect requires two past events, with the earlier one using "had + past participle"
    - "By the time" + past tense is a clear signal for past perfect in the following clause
    - Isolated time expressions without comparison to another past event use simple past
    - The structure "had been to" is always used with "to," never "had went"

### "After" vs "afterwards" usage

Clarification on the difference between these two similar words.

- Details
    
    - leticia.zach: Initially confused "afterwards" with "despite" or "even though"; selected "afterwards" in quiz but was uncertain
    - Be: Explained that "afterwards" means "depois" (after/later) and is not related to "even though"; clarified that "after" is followed by a noun or clause (after dinner, after I meet you), while "afterwards" stands alone and typically requires a comma; provided examples: "I like to eat candies after dinner" vs "I will have dinner, afterwards I will eat candies"
- Conclusion
    
    - "After" is used with a following noun or clause
    - "Afterwards" is used independently and typically requires a comma
    - Both can be correct in some contexts but have different grammatical structures
