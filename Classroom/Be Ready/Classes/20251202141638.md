## Overview

This English language class focused on teaching advanced grammatical structures, particularly the use of present perfect tense with "already," "yet," and "still," as well as causative structures with "have something done" and "have someone do something." The lesson began with reviewing homework and practicing these structures through various examples and exercises. Later, the class covered a listening exercise about street crime prevention advice from a police website.

- Present perfect tense with time markers (already, yet, still)
- Causative structures: "have something done" and "have someone do something"
- Past participle forms of irregular verbs
- Crime prevention vocabulary and safety advice

- When do we use "yet" versus "already" in sentences?

## Key Takeaways and Summary of Learning Objectives

- Present perfect tense is used with specific time markers: "already" in affirmative sentences, "yet" in negative sentences and questions
- The structure "have something done" is used when you pay someone to perform a service for you
- The structure "have someone do something" is used when you order, convince, or make someone perform an action
- Irregular verbs follow specific patterns in their past participle forms
- Being aware of your surroundings and taking preventive measures can help avoid becoming a victim of street crime

## Topic 1: Present Perfect with Time Markers

The class began with practicing the present perfect tense, particularly focusing on the use of time markers like "already," "yet," and "still." The teacher emphasized that "already" is typically used in affirmative sentences (e.g., "I've already worked on Saturday"), while "yet" is used in negative sentences and questions (e.g., "I haven't sent it to you yet," "Have you made dinner yet?"). Students practiced forming these sentences through guided examples, with the teacher correcting common mistakes. The lesson highlighted that these time markers don't always have direct translations between languages, which can cause confusion for learners. The teacher also explained that contractions like "I've not" are grammatically correct but sound unnatural compared to "I haven't."

### Relevant Q&A

Caleb: Why can't I use "still" in negative sentences?

Be: You don't use "still" in negatives. You use "still" in affirmatives, and "yet" in negatives and questions.

## Topic 2: Causative Structures

The class covered two important causative structures: "have something done" and "have someone do something." The first structure is used when you pay someone to perform a service for you (e.g., "I had my hair cut," "I had my car repaired"). The second structure is used when you order, convince, or make someone perform an action (e.g., "I had my brother drink 2 liters of water," "I had my boss give me a raise"). The teacher used visual aids to illustrate these structures and provided multiple examples to help the student understand the differences between them. The lesson emphasized that in the "have something done" structure, the past participle form of the verb is used, while in the "have someone do something" structure, the base form of the verb is used.

### Relevant Q&A

Caleb: I have my brother drink 2 liters of water.

Be: That's correct for making someone do something. But if you're talking about the past, you would say "I had my brother drink 2 liters of water."

## Topic 3: Crime Prevention Advice

The final part of the class involved a listening exercise about street crime prevention. Students listened to advice from a police crime prevention website and had to identify the missing words in various safety recommendations. The advice included: be prepared, be assertive, be aware, hide valuables, go against the flow, trust your instincts, make a plan, and look out for trouble. Each piece of advice was explained in detail, with practical tips on how to stay safe in public spaces. The exercise helped students practice listening comprehension while learning useful vocabulary related to safety and crime prevention.

### Relevant Q&A

Caleb: What does "be aware" mean?

Be: It means to be conscious of your surroundings, to be alert and paying attention to what's happening around you. Being "self-aware" means being conscious of yourself, like noticing when you're feeling nervous.
