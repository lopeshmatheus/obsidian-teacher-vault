### English article usage (a/an)

The teacher explained when to use "a" versus "an" in English.

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that "an" is used before vowel sounds to make pronunciation flow better
    - Be: Demonstrated examples like "an American," "an English teacher," "a European"
    - Be: Clarified that it's about the sound, not the spelling (e.g., "a university" but "an umbrella")
    - Rafael: Practiced using articles with various words
- Conclusion
    
    - The rule is based on pronunciation, not spelling
    - "An" is used before vowel sounds to make words flow together better
    - It's easier to assume "a" is the default and switch to "an" when it sounds awkward

### Pronunciation practice

The teacher helped Rafael with specific pronunciation challenges in English.

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that "ey" letters are usually pronounced as "ee" (key, monkey, money, Disney)
    - Be: Demonstrated silent letters in words like "psychology" and "tsunami"
    - Be: Showed how "eu" is pronounced as "you" (Europe, European)
    - Rafael: Practiced pronouncing these challenging words
- Conclusion
    
    - English pronunciation often differs from spelling
    - Some letters are silent in certain words
    - Imported words from other languages may have adapted pronunciations

### Workspace vocabulary

They reviewed vocabulary related to workspaces and practiced using "have got" structure.

- Details
    
    - Be: Introduced workspace-related words: keyboard, charger, headphones, chair, earbuds, laptop, screen, picture, plant, notebook, lamp, pen
    - Rafael: Identified items in his wife's workspace including chair, screen, keyboard, desk, pens, lamp
    - Be: Explained the difference between "have" and "have got" (both correct but "have got" is more informal)
    - Rafael: Practiced forming sentences with "have got" and "haven't got"
- Conclusion
    
    - Rafael has a good understanding of workspace vocabulary
    - The "have got" structure should be pronounced as one unit without pausing between words

### Countable vs. uncountable nouns

The teacher explained the difference between countable and uncountable nouns in English.

- Details
    
    - Be: Explained that some nouns have both singular and plural forms (dog/dogs, car/cars)
    - Be: Demonstrated that other nouns don't change form (rice, coffee, money, time)
    - Be: Showed that countable nouns use "a/an" in singular form
    - Rafael: Practiced identifying countable and uncountable nouns
- Conclusion
    
    - Countable nouns can be singular (with "a/an") or plural (with "s")
    - Uncountable nouns don't change form and don't use "a/an"
    - Learning which nouns are countable vs. uncountable requires practice and observation
